On Attachment Theory and the Thermodynamics of Peace

We often mistake peace for a tensionless state—a placid equilibrium devoid of conflict or uncertainty. However, from the perspective of non-equilibrium thermodynamics, a completely tensionless state where entropy has reached its maximum is not peace; it is thermodynamic death.

Living systems, and healthy human relationships, inherently demand energy to maintain order against the constant pull of chaos. True peace is not the absence of tension. I argue that peace is a highly active, high-energy state where each individual subsystem voluntarily bears its own share of entropy without venting it into the shared environment.

I. The Corollary of the Unknowable

To understand interpersonal dynamics, we must first accept a fundamental epistemological limit: The corollary to "Not Knowable" is "Not Controllable."

When faced with the sheer unpredictability of another human being, our egos violently resist the unknowable. We demand that all variables be quantifiable because if a system can be understood, it can theoretically be controlled. To demand that a partner's future feelings, actions, or attachment shifts be computable is an attempt to sit at the control panel of reality itself.

Learning to operate in situations where the parameters are entirely out of your control requires the capacity to sit in the dark, feel the pressure of the unknown, and consciously decide not to move, judge, or act.

II. The Mathematics of Relational Entropy

I believe this concept is not merely a philosophical metaphor; it maps precisely onto the laws of thermodynamics.

Consider a relationship as an isolated thermodynamic system $U$, composed of two local subsystems (individuals) $A$ and $B$. The total entropy of the relationship is the sum of the local entropies plus the entropy of their interaction space: $$S_{U} = S_{A} + S_{B} + S_\text{interaction}$$ According to the Second Law of Thermodynamics, the total entropy change of the isolated system must continually increase or remain constant:$$\Delta S_{U} \ge 0$$ When subsystem $A$ experiences internal emotional tension (anxiety, fear of abandonment, uncertainty), its local entropy $S_{A}$ spikes. At this juncture, $A$ has two choices:

  • Containment (True Peace): $A$ utilizes internal energy to tolerate and process the uncertainty. $\Delta S_{A}$ increases locally, but no chaotic emotional heat ($Q$) is transferred to $B$. The global system remains structurally intact.
  • Projection (Venting): $A$ refuses to bear the internal tension and attempts to alleviate local pressure by projecting it outward—through blame, demanding reassurance, or utilizing avoidant deactivating strategies.

Mathematically, when $A$ projects this internal chaos, it transfers "heat" to $B$ through a highly irreversible, non-equilibrium process. Because emotional friction is irreversible, the entropy generated in the interaction space and within subsystem $B$ will always exceed the temporary relief gained by $A$. Following Clausius's theorem:$$\Delta S_{interaction} > \left| \Delta S_{A_{relief}} \right|$$ Therefore, the inequality dictates that whenever a local subsystem refuses to bear its own uncertainty and exports it outward, the total relational entropy $\Delta S_{U}$ inevitably and disproportionately increases. Exporting local chaos strictly accelerates global relational decay.

III. Ego Leaks: Computing the Uncomputable

When our attachment systems are triggered, we often fail to contain our local entropy. Instead, our ego attempts to hack the system to ensure a favorable outcome. Even when we believe we have surrendered, the ego often leaks energy through covert computations:

  • The "Dosage of Presence" Computation: Attempting to calculate the exact behavioral algorithm (whether to smile, say hi, or remain stoic) to elicit a specific realization of value from a former partner. If you have truly surrendered to the unknowable, their perception of your output becomes a dead variable.
  • Modeling Their Entropy (Retroactive Justice): Running background simulations on a partner's future psychological state. The ego computes the trajectory of their future regret because it demands a balanced equation: I am suffering now, therefore they must suffer later to validate my reality. True containment means shutting down their simulation entirely.
  • The Retrospective Bug Hunt: Scanning relationship logs for an error code you might have written. The ego hates the concept of an unsolvable problem and prefers self-blame over admitting a lack of control. It resists the terrifying truth: You can run the correct code, execute the right boundaries, and offer a secure attachment, and the system will still crash if the other hardware is fundamentally incompatible.
  • The "Temporary Hold" Illusion: Enduring the current tension only under the assumption that there is a guaranteed payout (a better connection) at the end of the timeline. True peace requires accepting that the tension of uncertainty might be the permanent operating state, with no guaranteed reward, and choosing to hold your structure regardless.

IV. Kenosis

To step away from these computational illusions requires an active self-emptying, known in theology as Kenosis.

Humbling oneself—or "letting it be"—is frequently misunderstood as passive resignation. In reality, emptying the ego means taking the immense cognitive energy previously wasted on computing an uncomputable future and redirecting it into reinforcing your own vessel. It is letting the ultimate algorithms of reality govern the system instead of your own.

This framework brings absolute clarity to the ancient wisdom of Proverbs 31: "Charm is deceptive, and beauty is fleeting; but a woman who fears the Lord is to be praised." Charm and beauty are mechanisms of manipulation. They are assets deployed to bypass uncertainty, elicit guaranteed reactions, and control the interpersonal environment. Conversely, "fearing the Lord" is the structural acceptance of human epistemological limits. The individual who operates from this stance does not need to rely on depreciating, manipulative tools because they possess the structural integrity to sit in the unknowable without forcing an outcome. Their praise is earned through their capacity to bear reality exactly as it is.

V. Attachment Styles as Thermodynamic Sensitivities

I argue that attachment styles are fundamentally a reflection of how we manage thermodynamic pressure. Broadly, these styles fall into two categories: secure and insecure.

Within the insecure spectrum, we find two primary branches: anxious (characterized by a profound fear of abandonment) and avoidant (characterized by an intense fear of vulnerability). The avoidant branch further splits into two distinct operational modes:

  • The Dismissive-Avoidant: Values independence above all else and willingly dismisses anyone who threatens this isolated equilibrium.
  • The Fearful-Avoidant (FA): A volatile combination of both anxious and avoidant programming. This is the individual who craves deep connection and intimacy, but panics and pushes away when the relationship begins to deepen. They are trapped in a paradox: they fear being alone, yet equally fear being too close to someone.

To understand these mechanics, we must look at the specific types of entropy (uncertainty) they are interacting with. There is massive entropy in being alone: the combinatorial explosion of potential partners, infinite timelines, and undefined futures. But there is also terrifying entropy in being vulnerable: the combinatorial explosion of the other person's reaction once they see who you really are. When you drop your "socially validated persona," the outcome becomes radically uncertain. They might leave you, judge you, hurt you, or hate you—or they might accept you, love you, and admire you.

Each attachment style exhibits different degrees of sensitivity to these varying types of entropy, desperately trying to reduce one form of chaos at the cost of another. Ultimately, attachment styles boil down to a single question: Which type of entropy, over which time frame, are you unwilling to bear?

Even the secure attachment style wants to reduce entropy, but they are willing to do so over a much longer timeframe. The initial phase of waiting, dating, and exploring requires an incredibly high tolerance for the uncertainty of vulnerability. However, after bearing this tension and finding a stable partner with whom to build a secure relationship, the baseline entropy of the system drops completely.

In physical chemistry, this is exactly analogous to activation energy. It is the necessary spike in energy, tension, and volatility required before a covalent bond is formed and the molecular system achieves a stabilized, lower-energy state. Insecure styles refuse to pay this activation energy—either by fleeing the reaction entirely (avoidant) or trying to force the bond without proper alignment (anxious).

VI. The Earned Secure: Tolerating the Weight of Uncertainty

An Avoidant cannot tolerate the entropy of deep intimacy; when faced with a stable, low-entropy partner, they force a system crash to relieve their internal pressure. An Anxious attacher attempts to control the uncomputable by bleeding their entropy onto their partner through demands for reassurance.

True security—an Earned Secure attachment—is not the absence of internal chaos. It is the emotional muscle built by enduring it. It is the sheer, brute-force capacity to lift the weight of pure, unadulterated uncertainty, hold it in the air, and refuse to hurl it aside when your fibers begin to tear. I believe that this is one of the most fundamental aspect of human intelligence; not our ability to compute, predict, or control, but rather our capacity to endure the unknowable. And we are not born with this capacity. It is a muscle that we train with repeated exposure to the tension of uncertainty, and it is strengthened by the presence of a secure entity who can hold space for us while we build this emotional resilience.

Thus, a peaceful system is a system where global entropy is controlled by each local subsystem's willingness to bear its share of entropy to the best of its ability. It requires dropping the need to win the narrative, stepping away from the control panel, and actively holding back the chaos so the system can simply exist. It requires a dedication of every individual subsystem to the health of the global system, even at the cost of their own local, short-term relief.
